## 1b. How far the findings reach

Reproducing a finding once, at one setting, with one set of functional forms, is
weak evidence — the standing objection to any formal model in this field is that
its results are artifacts of its shapes. So each finding above is now quantified
over a **class** of forms rather than the one we picked: 100 combinations drawn
from three declared families (sigmoid credibility, concave saturation, convex
cost), across 24 parameter settings. Full table in [SCOPE.md](SCOPE.md);
regenerate with `make scope`.

The result is a clean inversion, and it favours you:

| | Robust to shape? | Robust to setting? |
|---|---|---|
| **Your three comparative statics** | **every shape, without exception** | 75–100% |
| Our drawn geometry (MB starts at origin, channels stay non-negative) | 73–74% | every setting |

**What you asserted does not depend on our functional forms at all.** Principal
costs sparing the control channel, and agency costs hitting both, hold for
*every* combination at *every* setting tested — they follow from the incidence
rules, which are structural, and not from any choice of curve. Soft governance
raising B holds under every shape too, and on 75% of settings.

**What we drew does depend on them.** MB starting at the origin holds for only
74% of shapes; a steep-at-the-origin saturation (`cbrt`, 27%) breaks it. Both
channels staying non-negative holds for 73%; the most convex cost shape breaks it
at 14%. So the fine geometry of the inverted U is our arithmetic, while the
comparative statics are your theory.

That is the distinction worth having, and it is now measured rather than asserted.

**One correction it forced on us.** Two findings first came back at exactly 75%
for every member of every family — the signature of a mis-stated claim, not a
fragile one. The comparative statics had been written as absolute shocks
("competence → 0.30"), which silently inverts wherever the sampled baseline
already sits below that. Restated as relative shocks, both are universal. The
scope analysis caught an error in how we had stated your result.

## 2. What does not reproduce, and why it cannot

Sheet 1 draws c(π) rising monotonically and staying under MB(π) all the way to
where MB returns to zero. **Those cannot both be true.**

> If c ≤ MB at π\*, and MB(π\*) = 0, then c(π\*) ≤ 0.
> If c is also rising and non-negative, then 0 ≤ c(0) ≤ c(π\*) ≤ 0.
> So c ≡ 0 and **B ≡ 0** — the control channel is empty.

This is geometry, not a bad choice of functional form: no algebra escapes it. I
searched 2,304 parameterisations before working out why none of them worked.

Three ways out. **We implemented the second**, which I think is what you meant:

1. c rises then falls, peaking with MB. The decomposition holds throughout, but
   the boundary is not monotone.
2. **The picture stops at the crossing π̄ < π\*.** c stays monotone; at π̄ the
   control channel absorbs the whole marginal benefit and the residual channel is
   exhausted. Here **π̄ = 0.54**.
3. MB never returns to zero — but then there is no interior optimum in total
   value, and the tool has nothing to tell a board.

Reading 2 makes π̄ a real quantity worth naming: **the level of purpose
integration at which everything the firm concedes has become contractible, and
nothing is left to discretion.** That is a board-relevant number the sketch
gestures at without isolating.

## 3. Two things that change the specification

**The substitutable trio is not a trio.** `notes/16-model-spec.md` proposes three
mutually substitutable mechanisms — purpose vulnerability reduction, AI
contractibility, absence of principal conflict — as the demonstration case for
the paper's technical contribution. Running the full ablation lattice:

| Ablated | Δ peak value |
|---|---|
| purpose route only | −0.287 |
| AI route only | −0.056 |
| **both routes** | **−0.618** ← far worse than the sum, the signature of substitution |
| principal conflict (alone, or with either, or with both) | +0.056 in every case |

The first two are genuine substitutes and single-mechanism ablation is blind to
them, so **the ablation-lattice argument survives intact**. But the third is a
*gate*, not a substitute: with no principal conflict there is nothing to protect
against, so it makes the other two irrelevant and every subset containing it
gives the same answer. The demonstration case is a substitutable **pair under a
condition**, and the spec should say so.

**AVp is a difference, and differences can mislead.** A mechanism that operates at
π = 0 raises the V₀ baseline as well as the peak. The AI protection route raises
value at every π, yet *lowers* measured AVp — so an ablation study reading AVp
alone reports it as harmful. The model now exposes `Vp`, the level, alongside
`AVp`. Worth knowing before we interpret any result off the loop.

| 4 | **The 2 × 3 grid**, your A and B as column sums | Low. It recovers your sketch exactly as a marginal. |

**The one question I most need answered.** Sheet 1 labels A *residual rights* and B
*control rights*. In Stoelhorst & Vishwanathan, "residual" is a modifier rather than a
category — their space is a 2 × 2 of (control | claim) × (fixed | residual) — so two
readings are open:

> **Is A residual _control_ rights — the discretion the board keeps — with B the part that
> has been contractually specified? Or did you mean residual _claims_, against control?**

Everything here assumes the first, and the model forces it: contractibility moves value
from A to B as more can be specified ex ante, and AI raises it because more can be written
down and verified. That is the fixed/residual axis. Under the second reading the whole α
mechanism is incoherent.

The first reading is also the stronger position — it makes the model extend the
*distinctive* move of your anchor paper, which is taking fixed control rights seriously
rather than following modern property rights theory's focus on residual ones.

But it has a consequence worth your view: **there is then no claim-rights channel at all.**
We implement the space of their design principle 1 (control rights against shirking and
externalities) and not principle 2 (claim rights against hold-up and market power).
Shareholders are in the model; the mechanism S&V uses to protect them is not. A third
channel is the obvious extension, and it is not in scope for November.

If you meant the second reading, it is the contractibility mechanism that needs rethinking,
not the model — and better to know now than in October.

## 4b. A second tension in the sketch, found the same way

Your x-axis reads **"purpose integration or lock-in"**, and sheet 2 panel 1 says soft
governance raises **B**. We implemented lock-in as a cost — what has been contractually
fixed cannot be adapted — and those two annotations turn out to conflict.

| cost of lock-in | soft governance → B | AI intensity → total value |
|---|---|---|
| none | **↗ as you drew it** | monotone up |
| moderate | ↗, barely | interior optimum, but its location is unstable |
| substantial | **↘ — your panel 1 reverses** | monotone down |

If lock-in is costly enough to matter, widening contractibility stops enlarging the control
channel, because what it adds in reach it loses in adaptability.

**We have not baked this in.** The model as you see it has no lock-in cost, so all three of
your comparative statics stand. But the mechanism is half of your own axis label, and
whether it belongs is your call rather than ours:

> **How costly is lock-in, in your intent? Enough to reverse panel 1, or a second-order
> effect the sketch sets aside?**

Full working in `notes/17-alpha-precheck.md`.
