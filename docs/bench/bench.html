Action Model Generators for Organization Science

Organization science produces findings faster than it builds on them. Theories multiply, and studies are hard to compare (Leavitt et al., 2010) (Durand et al., 2017). AI is pushing the number of papers up and their quality down, and the editors of Organization Science trace the recent rise in submissions primarily to AI use Gartenberg et al., 2026. The AMG Workbench uses AI the other way: to make knowledge cumulative.

It turns a research stream into an Action Model Generator (AMG): a map of the relationships the literature claims and tests around an outcome of interest. Think of an AMG as a meta-paper. Its relationships are what we used to call a theoretical model, and checking where each one holds is what we used to call empirical testing.

The name echoes Starbuck’s (1983) organizations as action generators. Scholars draw many action models from one AMG, and their findings are meant to inform what organizations do. The demonstration uses corporate purpose, a recent literature whose authors still define it differently.

The four phases

    Design principles

    1. Human-led. The scholar decides every input choice that builds the core model, drawing on what a language model proposes. Nothing proposed is used until you accept it, and every choice is recorded. (Bechky & Davis, 2025) (Tröbinger et al., 2026)
    2. AI-assisted. A language model can guide your thinking or take on whole tasks, such as reading a paper into the corpus. The mark shows where it works, and every exchange is stored with the prompt that produced it, so an ask can be audited and run again. (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021) (Carlson & Burbano, 2026)
    3. Traceable. Every element of the synthesis has a declared origin: each construct and relationship to the paper and passage behind it, each choice to whoever made it. (Groth et al., 2010)
    4. Modular. Each part of the method can be replaced on its own, or handed to a tool you already use: Scopus, Web of Science or OpenAlex for the literature; SymPy, Mathematica or LaTeX for models; R or Python for data. (Hucka et al., 2003)
    5. Testable. You choose the shape of each relationship, whether read off the literature or proposed as an exploratory hypothesis, and which method checks it. Each finding comes with the conditions under which it holds, and when the workbench cannot tell, it says so. (Davis et al., 2007) (Csaszar, 2020) (Tranchero et al., 2026) (He & Puranam, 2026)
    6. Open and reproducible. The code is under the MIT licence, derivation by proof is deterministic, and every decision is recorded, so an AMG documents the whole path from the literature it selected to the findings and their validity domains. Anyone can rerun it and get the same result. (Nosek et al., 2015) (Nosek et al., 2022)
    7. Standard and flexible. Every AMG follows the same frame and the same file format, so each becomes a building block that can be assembled with others. Within that frame the modelling choices are yours. (Grimm et al., 2020)

    The i beside a heading says why the workbench asks what it asks. Nothing is uploaded: every project stays in this browser until you export it.

    What this makes. An action model is what leaves here: a formal model of a literature, with every choice the literature left open declared rather than defaulted. Read on

    What this makes. An action model carries the arithmetic that closes the model, and each conclusion carries the region of the declared space its sign was proved on. It is called that because it is meant to be acted on — by a scholar who disagrees with one of its declarations and changes it, or by somebody deciding something. The eight steps build one; step 4.2 shares it as a building block, and step 1.1 is where somebody else’s would come in.

    This mark is on every place a language model is asked or has proposed something. Which model proposes. Wherever this page offers to fill something in, what answers is the model the host provides — on claude.ai that is Claude, and the page cannot name it, because the capability it asks does not say. It is written as an LLM throughout because nothing downstream depends on which one it is. Every proposal is parsed against the declared ids and the closed vocabularies before it is shown, anything outside them is counted rather than written, the record says which of the two of you chose each declaration, and the same path accepts an answer typed in by hand or returned by Mathematica. A wrong proposal is refused by name rather than argued with, so the model is replaceable and not merely unnamed — which is the property that matters, since infrastructure other researchers cannot reach is infrastructure they cannot check.

    What is on the map. A construct is a quantity one or more papers named, carried with its signature: level, scale, bearer, stock or flow. A relationship is one paper’s assertion that one construct acts on another, with its sign, form and owning theory. An arrow is every relationship running between two constructs, weighted by how many and coloured by the direction they agree on. A statement is what a relationship asserts, held apart from who asserted it, so several papers stating the same one are its evidence. A merge is the judgement that two constructs measure the same quantity.

    Why merging is done by hand. The unit is the nanopublication’s: Groth, Gibson & Velterop (2010) built the format so an aggregator could combine the several accounts of one statement — which rests on a registry giving every concept one identifier. This literature has none. So merging is the only operation that changes how much evidence a statement has, and it does not only raise it: unmerged, 22 of 597 statements carry more than one paper (3.7%); at step 1.2’s default, 22 of 164 (13.4%); merged harder, 22 of 152, the most this corpus yields; harder still, 7 of 29, because collapsing two constructs can delete the statement between them instead of pooling it. That is the argument for a curator and against a threshold.

    Where the bench stops. Steps 1.1 to 1.3 do the first step of Borsboom et al. (2021) — identify the phenomena, “general, robust and stubborn” where data is “particular, pliable and ephemeral” — which is what the corroboration counts are there to let you see. Step 2.1 is their prototheory and formal model. Their fourth step, checking the model reproduces the phenomena, needs those phenomena in the model’s own language; reconcile.py is the finding that this corpus does not supply it. Their fifth, weighing parsimony and plausibility, the bench does not do. Both are said here rather than hidden.
